## The left-digit effect

Shoppers read prices from left to right and give the first digit outsized weight. $2.99 feels closer to $2 than to $3, even though it is a cent away from $3. Research in consumer psychology has repeatedly found that prices ending in 9 can lift sales when the change alters the leftmost digit — the drop from $3.00 to $2.99 matters more than the drop from $3.60 to $3.59.

## Charm prices signal value; round prices signal quality

Prices ending in .99 or .95 tend to signal a deal. Round prices such as $100 tend to signal confidence and quality, and are easier to process — which suits luxury goods and emotional purchases. Many premium brands avoid .99 endings for exactly this reason.

## Precise numbers feel researched

In negotiations and estimates, precise figures ($4,975) are often read as better-informed than round ones ($5,000), and can anchor the other side more firmly. Use precision when you want to signal that a number was calculated, not guessed.

## Anchoring and decoys

The first number a customer sees becomes a reference point. Showing a higher-priced option first makes the middle option feel reasonable. A deliberately less attractive “decoy” option can steer choice toward a target plan — a pattern widely visible on subscription pricing pages.

## Ethics

All of these effects work on perception, not product. Use them to present honest value clearly, never to hide total cost. Transparent pricing builds the repeat customers that matter more than any single conversion.
